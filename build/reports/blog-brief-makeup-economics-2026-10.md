# Blog brief — what a make-up class actually costs you

**Proposed for:** October / November 2026
**Audience:** owners of children's activity businesses — swim schools, dance, music, languages, sports clubs, franchise networks. Written to be useful to someone who has never heard of Zooza.
**Status:** topic proposal, not drafted yet.

## The topic

How generous should your make-up policy be, and what is it costing you?

Not a product post. Nothing in it requires Zooza, or any software. It is a business piece about the one policy almost every activity provider sets emotionally and never prices.

## Why this one

Measured across four weeks of customer conversations, 12 September to 9 October 2026:

| Theme | Customer messages |
|---|---|
| Pricing and discounts | 72 |
| **Make-ups, cancellations, capacity** | **72** |
| Trials and converting them | 68 |
| Chasing payment | 58 |
| Instructors | 36 |

Make-ups tie for first, and they are raised by **38 different providers in 37 conversations** — the broadest spread of any theme. Pricing questions cluster in a handful of accounts setting things up; make-ups come from everybody, all the time.

What they are actually asking, in order:

| Sub-question | Conversations |
|---|---|
| **How many should I allow?** | 14 |
| No slot offered even though seats are free | 10 |
| Waiting list for the popular slots | 9 |
| I end up doing it by hand | 6 |
| Credit expired / should it carry over | 4 |

The top one is not a software question. It is a business question, and nobody has given them a way to answer it. That is the gap the post fills.

**Timing.** October is when it bites: the season has started, the first round of illness has arrived, and make-up requests peak. A provider reading this in late October is living it.

## The angle

> Everybody asks us how many make-up classes they should allow. Nobody can answer it for you — but you can work it out, and most people have never tried.

The insight the post is built on: **a make-up class is a seat you give away, and a seat is the only thing you sell.** Your revenue is seats × price × how full you are. A make-up policy is therefore a discount — one you grant automatically, without deciding its size, to whoever asks.

The honest other half, which keeps the piece from being mean-spirited: **make-ups also retain people.** A parent who misses three paid classes with no recourse does not renew. For some businesses the policy is the cheapest retention spend they have. The post is not "stop being generous" — it is "know what you are buying."

That tension is what makes it worth reading rather than a list of tips.

## Outline

1. **The line item nobody budgets for.** Open on the October reality: the messages start arriving, you say yes to each one, and by December you have no idea how many free classes you gave away.
2. **What one make-up costs — three situations.** Simple arithmetic, no spreadsheet needed.
   - The class has empty seats → it costs you almost nothing. Say yes.
   - The class is full → the make-up displaces a seat you could have sold, or pushes you over your instructor ratio. This is the expensive one.
   - You run extra capacity for make-ups → you have pre-paid for it, so the question becomes whether it is sized right.
3. **How "be generous" compounds.** Credits that roll into next term, make-ups of make-ups, the slots everyone wants and nobody can get into, and the admin hours — which are the real cost for most small providers and never appear anywhere.
4. **Four policy shapes, and who each suits.**
   - Unlimited within the term — simple, generous, works when you are not full
   - A ratio (one make-up per N sessions) — scales across weekly and twice-weekly customers
   - A window (use it within N days) — protects next term's capacity
   - Paid make-ups — unpopular to say out loud, normal in adult fitness
5. **When a make-up is the cheapest save you will ever make.** The retention argument, honestly made, with the cases where saying yes is obviously right.
6. **Write your policy in six lines.** A fill-in-the-blanks template the reader can publish on their own site this week.
7. **Three numbers to watch.** Make-ups granted per 100 enrolments, share used vs expired, and how full your make-up-heavy slots run.

## What only we can say

We see this across hundreds of providers in several countries, which lets the post make claims no single business could:

- That the most common question is "how many", and that almost nobody has a number
- That providers converge on roughly the same few policy shapes independently
- One telling anecdote, anonymised: a provider so swamped by make-up requests that they deliberately restricted make-ups to a period containing no sessions, so parents would open the booking page and find nothing. That is not a feature, it is a cry for help — and it is a perfect illustration of a policy nobody designed.

Use patterns, never named businesses, and no figures that could identify an account.
